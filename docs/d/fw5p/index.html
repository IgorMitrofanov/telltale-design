The Night Shift at Pier 9

Between ten at night and six in the morning, four hundred people empty a ship the size of a street. Almost nobody who ordered what is inside it will ever see them.

At twenty to eleven the last of the day crew walks out through the gate at Pier 9 with their helmets under their arms, and the people walking in the other direction do not say much. Nobody has to explain the arrangement. The Maren Sofie, a 9,400-box feeder out of Rotterdam, has been alongside since four in the afternoon and is still two-thirds full. By morning she must be empty, refilled, and gone, because the tide, the tug schedule and a customs window on the far side of the North Sea do not care who is tired.

Pier 9 is the oldest working berth at the port of Calder Reach, a long concrete tongue pushed out into the estuary in 1964 for a type of cargo that no longer exists. Its lighting, forty-one masts of sodium-orange swapped for white LED two winters ago, makes the whole place look like a stage that has been set for a play nobody is watching. Between ten at night and six in the morning the pier handles, on a decent night, about 4,600 tonnes an hour at its peak and 4,300 boxes in total. It does this with a crew of 412, which is fewer than worked the same berth in 1990 to move a tenth of the weight.

22:40The gate

Tomasz Wiśniewski has supervised the night shift for eleven years, and he begins each one the same way: by walking the length of the quay alone, before the briefing, with a paper plan folded in his back pocket. The plan is the ship’s stowage chart, a grid of coloured cells that says what is in which slot. Some cells are red, which means dangerous goods; some are blue, which means refrigerated and therefore on a clock of its own. He does not trust the screen version, he says, because a screen cannot be written on with a thumbnail.

“People think the work is lifting,” he says, at the foot of Crane 3, with the diesel hum of the yard tractors already building behind him. “The lifting is the easy bit. The work is knowing what has to come out first, so that nothing has to be moved twice.”

The lifting is the easy bit. The work is knowing what has to come out first.

Tomasz Wiśniewski, night supervisor

Above him, in a glass cab forty metres up, Margo Oyelaran is settling into a seat that was bolted in before she was born. She has driven this crane for nine years. The cab has a window in its floor, and the work consists largely of looking down through it at a steel box the size of a delivery van, hanging from four cables in a wind, and bringing it to rest on a trailer with a margin of about two centimetres. She does this, on average, once every ninety seconds, for eight hours. She says the secret is to stop thinking of the box as an object. “It’s a pendulum,” she says. “You’re not placing it. You’re persuading it.”

00:10Lashing

Midnight is when the pier is loudest. The first hour is a negotiation between machines; by ten past twelve the rhythm has set, and what you hear is a continuous sound with no single source, the noise of a very large animal breathing. This is also when the lashers go to work, and the lashers are the people the pier would be quickest to call its backbone, if anyone asked.

Containers stacked on a ship’s deck are held down by steel rods and twist-locks, and every one of them has to be released by hand before a crane can lift it, then fitted again for the next voyage. Dev Pillay, thirty-four, has done this work since he was nineteen. He climbs between stacks eight boxes high on ladders bolted to their corners, carrying a rod that weighs eleven kilos, and he does it in the dark with a headtorch, in weather. His hands are the first thing you notice. The skin across both palms is the colour and texture of a worn boot.

“Nobody thanks the lasher,” he says, without self-pity. “If I do my job right, the ship just sits there and the box just comes off. It’s only when it goes wrong that anyone knows my name.” He pauses to tighten a strap on a glove. “Which is fine. I have a quiet life.”

02:30The quiet hour

Somewhere around half past two the pier loses its nerve. It is not a dramatic change. The crane cycle lengthens by ten seconds. The radio chatter, which at midnight was a continuous stream of numbers, thins out into single words. This is the hour that the shift workers I spoke to were most reluctant to describe, and then least able to stop describing. The body wants to be asleep and the rest of the city is, and the pier is the one lit place for miles.

Ines Carvalho, who tallies boxes at the end of Crane 2, keeps a thermos of black tea and a rule that she will not look at the clock between two and three. “If you look, it moves slower,” she says. She has a daughter who is nine and who sees her mother on Sunday afternoons, awake and a little cross, like someone who has flown across several time zones to be there. “She calls it my jet lag,” Ines says. “I tell her it’s the same thing, only I never get to go anywhere.”

It’s a pendulum. You’re not placing it. You’re persuading it.

Margo Oyelaran, Crane 3 driver

04:15The count

By four the ship is hollow, the tops of the stacks gone and the cell guides showing like the ribs of a fish. Loading begins, and the work turns from taking things apart in the right order to building a ship that will balance, container by container, in the dark.

A mistake here is quiet but costly. A refrigerated unit left away from its power socket is a cargo of strawberries turned to jam by Hamburg. The tally clerks, Ines among them, are the last line of defence: they stand at the foot of the crane with a handheld scanner and read each seal number aloud, against the plan, before the box goes up. They are wrong, she estimates, once in about four thousand lifts. “Which sounds good,” she says, “until you do four thousand lifts in a week.”

05:50Handover

The first light comes up grey behind the grain silos on the far bank, and with it the day crew, arriving in twos and threes with the stiff cheerfulness of people who have slept. There is a short, practised ritual. Tomasz gives his plan to his counterpart, thumbs the red cells, names the one box that is still wrong. Margo climbs down the ladder, slowly, one hand at a time, and stands on the quay for a moment with her face tilted up as if she were checking the weather.

By six the Maren Sofie is riding high and then, over the next hour, low again, loaded for Gdańsk. The tugs come in. The ropes are slipped one at a time and fall into the black water, and the ship backs off the pier with a long tired sound from her horn. The night crew do not watch her go. They are already at the gate, helmets under their arms, passing the day shift going the other way, and still nobody says very much.

Dev stops at the kerb. He has a bus to catch and a flat across the river where the curtains are thick. Behind him the cranes swing their booms back to the vertical, one after another, like animals settling. “People ask if I mind never seeing the day,” he says. “I tell them I see the best of it. I see it start.”

Hannah Voss is a freelance writer based in Hull who covers ports, shipping and the people who work in them. Her first book, Slack Water, about the last working docks of the Humber, was shortlisted for the Coastal Book Prize. She spent six nights at Pier 9 in March and one, at the crew’s insistence, in the crane cab. Photographs by Ilya Brandt, who lives on a narrowboat and has not slept a full night since 2019.
