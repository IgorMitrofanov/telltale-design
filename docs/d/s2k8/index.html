The Night Shift at Pier 9

Between midnight and dawn, a few hundred people move a city’s worth of goods off ships you will never see. Nobody asked them to be invisible. It simply comes with the hours.

At ten to midnight the car park behind Pier 9 is nearly full, and nobody is talking. Forty-odd people stand around in hi-vis jackets the colour of boiled sweets, holding paper cups, looking at the sky the way farmers do. It is not the weather they are reading. It is the ship, a hull the length of three football pitches, creeping up the channel with her deck lights on and a tug leaning on each shoulder.

“That’s the Maren Skou,” says Delphine Okafor, who has run the night gang for eleven years and still counts the stacks aloud as the vessel comes round. “Fourteen across, nine high on the forward bays. She’s heavy. She’ll be late.” She is right on both counts. The first line goes ashore at 00:07, and by the time it is made fast the day shift’s radio chatter has been replaced by something quieter and flatter, the grammar of people who have done this a thousand times.

IThe hour the gate lifts

Port work was once a trade you inherited at the dock gate, a morning scrum for a brass tally. Now the gate is a turnstile and the tally is a thumbprint, but the sense of a threshold survives. Past it, the pier is a different country. The lights are sodium orange and white halogen, so bright over the stacks that the surrounding river looks painted on. Everything that is not a container is a shadow.

The night shift runs from 23:30 to 07:30, and it exists because ships do not care what time it is. A vessel that sits at anchor costs her owner about forty thousand pounds a day in charter, fuel and lost schedule. A berth that sits empty costs the port nearly as much. So the cranes run, as the saying here goes, until the water says otherwise.

Nobody sees the port at night. That is the whole job, really: to make sure that nobody has to.

Delphine Okafor, night operations lead

IIThe arithmetic of a box

A standard container is twenty feet long and, loaded, may weigh anything from two tonnes of cushions to twenty-eight tonnes of ceramic tile. It is a sealed secret. Nobody on the pier opens one unless a customs officer says so. What they know is the number on the side, the slot on the plan and the lean of the stack when the crane takes the strain.

On a good night Pier 9 moves roughly seven hundred boxes across the quay wall. The throughput is not even. It sags after midnight while the gangs find their rhythm, climbs through the small hours, falls apart briefly at the tea break, and then surges toward dawn, when everyone is tired enough to stop being careful and fast enough not to need to. Nobody planned that curve. The terminal’s analysts found it, rather as an astronomer finds a planet, by noticing what the numbers kept doing.

Ask the crew about it and they shrug. They say it is simply what the night does to a person. At 01:00 you are carrying the whole evening with you; at 03:00 your body has filed a complaint; by five, something odd arrives, a thin clarity, like cold water. “You stop thinking about being tired,” says one of the signallers, “and you start thinking about the box.”

IIIFive people, one lift

The choreography behind a single crane move involves more people than most visitors expect. Up in the cab, forty metres over the deck, the driver watches a screen as much as the steel. Below, a signaller in a bright jacket talks to her by radio and by hand. Two lashers on the ship’s deck cast off the twist-locks, long steel dogs that pin each box to the one beneath. A truck driver waits in the lane to take the box, and a checker with a tablet ticks off the number.

If any of the five is wrong, the lift stops. It stops in silence, usually, because shouting is for the amateurs. Marcus Teague, a lasher for nineteen years, wears a rubber band on his wrist that he snaps when he is about to lose focus. “I have a bruise there in every season,” he says, and lifts his sleeve. It looks like a bracelet.

If you do it right, a lift is the quietest thing in the world. Forty tonnes, and not a word.

Priya Nandakumar, crane driver, Pier 9

Priya Nandakumar has been driving the big boom for six years. She came to it from a Glasgow bus depot, where she learned to read a mirror before she learned to read a manifest. “It is the same trick,” she says. “You are steering something that is much bigger than you, through a space that is much smaller than it should be.” At the cab’s window the river is a black stripe. She keeps a thermos of ginger tea wedged between the joystick and the wall, and she does not look at the clock.

IVWhat the dark does

Studies of night workers tend to read like indictments, and most of the indictments are fair. Sleep debt, strained marriages, a cardiac risk the actuaries calculate to a decimal. The pier’s occupational nurse, Gwen Alderton, keeps a corkboard of leaflets about all of it, and a bowl of oranges that disappears every week.

But talk to the crews for long enough and a second account emerges, one that no study seems to have asked about. They talk about the city seen from the water at 04:00, when the first bakery windows light up on the far shore. They talk about the foxes that patrol the container yard, and the heron that stands on the fender of berth five. They talk about each other. A night gang is a small and peculiar family, held together by the fact that the rest of the world is asleep and cannot be rung.

“My daughter thinks I work in the moon,” Marcus says, grinning. “She’s seven. I have not corrected her.”

VSlack water

At 05:50 the sky behind the cranes goes from black to the colour of a bruise. The gang has two hundred boxes behind it and a hundred and twelve to go. The pace quickens. It has to, because the river has its own timetable, and the Maren Skou must leave on the ebb or wait for the next one. The tugs are already idling alongside, their crews watching the quay like men at a station.

The last lift, a blue box with a Rotterdam stencil, goes down at 06:58. Delphine marks it on her clipboard and, as she always does, writes the time in the corner in pencil. “I don’t know why,” she says. “Nobody reads them.” Then the first of the day shift appears at the gate, clean-jacketed and yawning in the wrong direction, and the night people hand over their radios.

They drift to the car park without ceremony. Somebody starts an engine. The ship slides out into a pale river with her decks neatly cleared, and by the time the commuters reach the bridge she is a grey shape on the estuary, no more remarkable than a cloud. Nobody on the bridge looks up. That, Delphine would say, is the point.
