The Night Shift at Pier 9

Between ten at night and six in the morning, one feeder ship gives up 764 boxes and 9,170 tonnes. This is who takes them down, and what it costs to do it in the dark.

The gate22:10

The Maren Gudrun came up the Calder on the evening flood with her bow riding low and every deck light burning, and by ten past ten the pilot had her alongside Pier 9. Two tugs leaned on her until the lines were fast. Nobody on the quay looked up. Most of them had watched a ship come in more than a thousand times, and the night’s work had started before the first rope. It had started at the gate.

Gate 3 opens to haulage trucks at ten. By the time the ship was tied up, the queue ran back past the weighbridge: thirty-one cabs with their engines ticking over. Each driver held a booking for one specific box, and each box was still stacked somewhere in the dark above the water. Ruth Adeyemi had to make the ship give those boxes up in an order the trucks could use. The ship had stowed them in a different order, for a different port.

Adeyemi has planned the night shift for eleven years. Her tools are a stowage plan printed on A1, three coloured pencils and a radio that crackles whenever a crane swings. Every box on the plan has an address of six digits: bay, row, tier. The box at 140282 sits in bay 14, row 02, tier 82, the first one above the hatch covers. “You learn to read it like a street map,” she said. “Then you learn the streets are lying, because somebody loaded them in Gdańsk in a hurry.” Reading the plan. Rows count outwards from the centreline: even numbers to port, odd to starboard, 00 down the middle. Tiers on deck start at 82. The drawing above is bay 14.

Twenty-eight moves an hour00:15

By midnight four cranes were working the ship, and the whole quay had settled into a beat. Dilnoza Rahimova drives crane 4. Her cab hangs thirty-eight metres above the apron and has a window in the floor, so that she can look straight down at the box she is holding. She has been in that seat for nineteen years. She keeps a thermos wedged against the console, which the maintenance manual forbids.

A good crane makes twenty-eight moves an hour, one every two minutes and nine seconds. A move is lift, swing, lower, release, return. It sounds slow until you stand under it. The spreader locks onto the four corner castings, a box that may weigh thirty tonnes comes off its seat, and for a few seconds the whole load hangs in the wind off the estuary, turning a few degrees on its own. Moves. One move is one container lifted from ship to quay. A 40-foot box and a 20-foot box each count as one, which is why tonnes, not moves, make the fairer measure of a night.

From up there, Rahimova told me, the ship looks like a field. You do not unload a field. You take one row, and then the next.

A box that swings, you see it. A box that is two tonnes heavier than the paperwork says, you only find out in the wire.

Dilnoza Rahimova, crane driver, crane 4

The paperwork is the problem. Declared weights are often wrong by a tonne or more, and a crane’s load cell is the last honest witness on the quay. Rahimova can hear a heavy box in the note of the hoist before the display agrees with her. She calls it in on the radio, the planner moves the box to a different stack, and the night goes on. It happens three or four times a shift.

The people on the deck01:50

Down on the ship, the work is done by hand. A gang of six lashers works the deck in head torches, a bay ahead of the cranes. Every box on deck sits on four twistlocks, one at each corner, and each lock has to be turned open by a person standing in a gap barely wider than their shoulders. Kwame Boateng has done this for twenty-three winters. He carries the locks in a canvas bag that weighs about eight kilos by the end of a bay. Twistlock. A steel fitting about the size of a fist that pins one box to the box below. It weighs four kilos. A seven-high stack on deck is held together by 24 of them.

The cold is the first problem and the order of work is the second. A lasher must clear the box the crane is about to take, then move before it swings. There is a rule that nobody stands under the spreader. There is a second rule, that nobody stands where the spreader will be in ninety seconds, and that one is harder to keep, because it depends on reading the driver.

A twistlock weighs four kilos. Forget to turn one and it is thirty tonnes deciding to come with you.

Kwame Boateng, lasher, deck gang 2

The gang checks in on the radio every twenty minutes. If a voice is missing, the crane stops. “Nobody takes it personally,” Boateng said. “The crane does not care whether you are fine. It only wants to hear it.”

Fifty minutes of nothing03:05

At 03:05 a breaker tripped in the quay substation. Cranes 1, 2 and 3 stopped where they were, spreaders in the air, and stayed that way for fifty minutes. Only crane 4 has its own feeder, so Rahimova went on lifting alone, one box every two minutes, while the quay went quiet around her.

Quiet is expensive. The trucks in the queue did not move faster. Adeyemi walked the line with her radio and a torch, telling drivers who would wait and who would go to the back lot, and she rewrote the plan in pencil on the A1 sheet. The deck gangs sat in the mess cabin, a steel box with a kettle, a microwave and a whiteboard where someone had drawn the ship as a rectangle with legs. “Nobody likes the quiet,” said Boateng. “You start to hear the water.”

When the breaker was reset at 03:55, the cranes made up some of the loss, though not all of it. The log shows what the night looked like: a plateau of about thirteen hundred tonnes an hour, a hole at three, and the plateau again.

What the morning gets05:30

The last lift was at 05:58. Seven hundred and sixty-four boxes had left the Maren Gudrun in seven and a half hours: tinned tomatoes, flat-pack wardrobes, a consignment of brake discs for a bus depot in Doncaster. Nobody who buys any of it will think about Pier 9. That is how the shift knows it has done its job.

By half past five the day gang was at the gate, drinking from paper cups and reading the log over Adeyemi’s shoulder. She wrote the handover in block capitals. Breaker, substation, 50 minutes. Boxes held for the morning, 12. Trucks gone, 29; two sent to the back lot. Then she did what she does at the end of every shift, and rubbed out the plan in pencil, so that the next night begins with a clean sheet.

Rahimova climbed down the crane’s ladder as the first commuter train crossed the swing bridge upriver, its windows full of people on their way to start. She does not take the train. She drives home the other way, against the traffic, and sleeps with the curtains shut until two.
