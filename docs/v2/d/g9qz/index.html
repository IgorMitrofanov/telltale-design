The Night Shift at Pier 9

From the last tide of the evening to the first bus of the morning, four hundred people empty a ship the size of a village. Almost none of the city that ordered what is inside will ever see them.

Times are for the night of 2 to 3 September 2026, Berth 9, Calder Reach.

22:40The tide, then the ship

The Halcyon Reach comes up the channel at twenty to eleven, riding so low that the pilot boat beside her looks like something left in a bath. She is 294 metres long and carries 6,412 containers from Busan by way of the Cape, and for nineteen days none of them has been touched by a human hand. On the quay at Pier 9, the first gang of forty, in orange, is waiting to change that.

Nobody cheers. A line handler walks out to the bollard with a lamp and a mooring rope as thick as his thigh. The ship, which has crossed two oceans, is told where to stop by a man in a reflective jacket holding a torch. By eleven the first gantry has swung out over her deck. By a quarter past, the boxes are moving.

The port authority says Pier 9 works around the clock, but the statement is generous about what that means. Daytime belongs to trucks and paperwork. The big ships, the ones with deadlines attached, are turned around at night, when the roads are empty, the berth fees are lower and the cranes are in nobody’s way. “The day is for people who need to talk to each other,” says Tomas Weir, who has run the lashing gangs for twenty-two years. “The night is for people who need to get on.”

00:15Twist-locks and cold hands

Weir’s gang is eleven strong, and its work is the least visible and the most dangerous on the pier. Every container on a ship is pinned to the one beneath it by steel twist-locks the size of a fist, and each one must be released by hand before the crane can lift. A ship this size carries some twenty thousand. The lashers climb the stacks in the dark on ladders welded to the bays, a pole in one hand, a head torch lighting roughly the next arm’s length.

“Four corners, four clicks. If you only hear three, you stop and you look.”

Dennis Achterberg, lasher, nineteen months on the gang

“You learn to count by feel,” says Dennis Achterberg, who is twenty-six and has been on the gang for nineteen months. “Four corners, four clicks. If you only hear three, you stop and you look.” Between midnight and two the cold comes straight off the water, and the hands go first. The gang drinks sweet tea from a flask passed from glove to glove. Most wear two pairs, thin under thick, so that they can strip the outer ones to work a latch without leaving skin on the steel.

Weir counts his night in near misses, not in boxes. He has a notebook for them, soft at the corners, and in twenty-two years it has filled three times. The last entry reads, in block capitals, wind got under a door, nobody hurt, ask crane to wait. The crane waited. That, he says, is the whole of the job.

02:00Forty metres up

Above them, in a glass box hung beneath the crane’s trolley, Lola Ferreira looks straight down between her boots. She has driven ship-to-shore cranes for fourteen years. The seat is a nest of joysticks, and the best window is in the floor. A container of up to thirty tonnes swings under her, and she must set it on a truck chassis only a hand’s width longer than the box, in the dark, in the wind, with a man standing beside the chassis who would like to go home with all his arms.

She makes one lift about every ninety seconds. At the peak of the night, a little after three, her crane moves eight hundred tonnes in an hour. The line then sags, twice: at one o’clock, when the gangs stop to eat, and again before dawn, when the body quietly gives its opinion of the whole enterprise.

“Around four I get a kind of tunnel,” Ferreira says. “The world goes down to a box and four corners. That is when I talk to myself, out loud. The radio operators know. Somebody always says, Lola, you are doing the voices again.”

03:50The tally

Somebody has to know where everything is. In a steel hut at the end of the pier, under a heater that smells of burnt dust, Hannah Okafor sits with a tablet and a radio and keeps the ship’s memory. Each box that leaves the hold gets a number, a slot in the yard, and a truck. If a number goes missing, a retailer in another country will be short of something by Friday.

The contents are a kind of poem she does not have time to read. In one night she has logged fourteen tonnes of hospital linen, a pallet of cello strings, two thousand garden gnomes, and a refrigerated box of prawns that must reach the market by eight. Refrigerated containers hum on the stack like sleeping animals; she hears them in her sleep.

Every box is somebody’s Tuesday. You only find out which one when it goes missing.

Hannah Okafor, tally clerk, nine years at Pier 9

Okafor says the job taught her that supply is not an abstraction. It is a woman in a hut at four in the morning making sure a hospital laundry has sheets. She says this plainly, between radio calls, without any sense that she has said something worth saying.

05:45The first bus

The sky over the estuary turns the grey of a wet slate. The day gang is arriving at the gate with coffee in paper cups, and the night gang is leaving it, and for a few minutes the two shifts pass each other without a word, the way trains do. Weir signs the last sheet. Eleven names, eleven signatures, one line at the bottom: all accounted for.

The Halcyon Reach sails on the 06:10 tide, lighter by 3,100 boxes and riding high enough to show a stripe of rusty red along her waterline, the colour of her own paint, which nobody sees until she is empty. The tugs lean on her. The pilot boat follows, small again.

At the stop outside the gate, the 05:52 bus is already full, and every passenger is going the other way, towards offices and classrooms and the ordinary morning that the night gang has just stocked for them. Weir finds a seat by the window. Ferreira stands. Achterberg, who is twenty-six, falls asleep before the first stop, his forehead against the glass, his hands still curled around a flask that has gone cold.

Marit Sandoval
